## 1. INDUSTRY AND COMPANY OVERVIEW


### 1.1  GLOBAL E-COMMERCE LANDSCAPE

Electronic commerce has moved in two decades from a convenience for a small number of urban buyers to the default way in which a large part of the world shops. The model itself has also changed. Early online retail was inventory-led: a company bought goods and sold them onward. The dominant model today is the marketplace, in which the platform does not own most of what it sells but connects independent sellers to buyers and earns from facilitating that exchange.

That shift matters because it changes what a platform has to be good at. An inventory-led retailer competes on buying well; a marketplace competes on the breadth and quality of its supply, the accuracy of its catalogue, the competitiveness of the prices its sellers offer and the reliability of fulfilment. Growth in a marketplace is therefore a business development activity as much as a technology activity, because every additional category and every additional seller has to be identified, approached and brought on to the platform.

Three global developments have shaped the sector recently. The first is the move of everyday consumption online, particularly groceries and fast-moving consumer goods, which are bought frequently in small quantities and were long considered unsuitable for e-commerce. The second is speed: rapid delivery models have compressed customer expectations from days to minutes, which in turn demands local supply and local sellers. The third is the professionalisation of the seller side, with platforms providing onboarding support, catalogue standards, pricing guidance and analytics to the businesses that sell through them. All three were visible in the work I did during this internship.


### 1.2  THE INDIAN E-COMMERCE CONTEXT

India is one of the fastest growing e-commerce markets in the world, and it is growing from a comparatively small base, which is what makes it attractive. The Indian e-commerce market is estimated at about USD 159.25 billion in 2026 and is projected to reach roughly USD 332.94 billion by 2031, a compound annual growth rate of close to 16 per cent (Mordor Intelligence, n.d.). Other estimates place the online retail market at about USD 229.77 billion in 2026, rising from USD 217.16 billion in 2025 (IMARC Group, n.d.-a), and the sector as a whole has been projected to nearly triple from about USD 125 billion in 2024 to USD 345 billion by 2030 (ETInfra, 2026).

Definitions differ, but the direction does not. Measured as gross merchandise value, Indian e-retail reached roughly USD 65 to 66 billion in 2025 and is expected to grow at more than twenty per cent a year to about USD 170 to 180 billion by 2030, at which point it would account for approximately one in every ten rupees of retail spending (ETRetail, 2026). Two features of that growth are important for a platform in this business: it is coming increasingly from customers outside the largest cities, and it is coming increasingly from everyday categories rather than from electronics.

The second point deserves emphasis because it defined the category I worked on. Grocery and fast-moving consumer goods have overtaken mobile phones as a major online segment, and the expansion of rapid delivery has been the main driver of that change (ETBrandEquity, 2026). For listed consumer goods companies, e-commerce now contributes roughly eight to fifteen per cent of domestic revenue, and between half and seventy per cent of that online revenue flows through quick commerce channels (HDFC Sky, 2026). Reported company data for FY 2026 put quick commerce at sixty to seventy-five per cent of total online sales for several large manufacturers (Economic Times, 2026), and one industry estimate suggests that around forty per cent of all FMCG consumption in India could take place through online channels by 2030 (Economic Times, 2025).

For sellers, this creates both an opportunity and a problem. The opportunity is obvious: demand is moving to channels where a small distributor can reach customers it could never have served physically. The problem is that listing on a platform requires product data, images, pricing discipline and a willingness to operate to somebody else’s catalogue standards, and many small FMCG sellers and distributors have neither the systems nor the staff for it. Bridging that gap, by researching categories, benchmarking prices, preparing listings and persuading sellers to come on board, is exactly the work an online selling platform needs done, and it is the work I was given.

The business-to-business side of Indian e-commerce illustrates the same logic at a larger scale. Platforms such as udaan, which was founded in 2016 and describes itself as India’s largest electronic business-to-business platform operating across FMCG, staples, fruits and vegetables and pharmaceutical categories (udaan, n.d.), together with Jumbotail and ElasticRun, have built their businesses by connecting manufacturers and distributors to small retailers. Their common insight is that the hard part is not the website; it is the supply side relationship.

### 1.4  STRATEGIC FOCUS AND CULTURE

For a young marketplace, strategy is essentially a sequence of choices about where to concentrate limited effort. The organisation’s focus during my internship was on building depth in selected FMCG categories rather than breadth across many, and on recruiting sellers and distributors who could supply those categories reliably. That focus is sensible: a marketplace with a thin catalogue in twenty categories is less useful to a customer than one with a complete catalogue in three.

The second element of the strategy is price credibility. In FMCG, the customer usually knows the price of the product, which means that a platform cannot be careless about it. This is why price comparison work recurred throughout the internship and why it was treated as an input to listing decisions rather than as a research exercise.


#### 1.4.1  VALUES IN PRACTICE

- **Accuracy of the catalogue:** product data is validated before it is used for onboarding or listing, because an error on the platform is visible to every customer who sees it.
- **Competitive pricing:** prices are benchmarked against retail and other platforms so that listings are credible rather than optimistic.
- **Relationships with sellers:** distributors and retailers are met in person, followed up professionally and taken through partnership discussions rather than merely sent a link.
- **Responsiveness on the platform:** defects found by any team member are reported to the development team and tracked, because a marketplace loses a customer quickly and regains one slowly.
- **Everybody does field work:** in a small organisation the person who builds a listing also visits the seller, which keeps the catalogue grounded in what sellers can actually supply.

#### 1.4.3  STRATEGIC POSITIONING

The Indian online selling market contains a small number of very large horizontal marketplaces, a set of well-funded quick commerce operators and a large number of smaller and newer platforms. Competing with the largest players on selection, delivery speed or discounting is not realistic for a young company. The available positions are narrower and more defensible: depth in particular categories, closeness to a specific seller community, or service to a regional market that the national platforms treat as secondary.

Q-Up’s positioning, as I observed it, rests on category depth in FMCG and on direct relationships with sellers and distributors built through field work. That is a slower way to grow than buying customers through advertising, but it produces supply that competitors cannot replicate by spending money, and it was the reason my internship consisted largely of field visits, vendor meetings and onboarding follow-up rather than desk work alone.

### 1.9  THE REGULATORY ENVIRONMENT FOR ONLINE MARKETPLACES

One feature of this industry deserves separate treatment, because it constrains what a marketplace may do and therefore what its supply-side team is actually selling to a prospective seller. An online platform in India operates within a regulatory framework that distinguishes sharply between owning inventory and providing a marketplace.

The foundational distinction comes from foreign investment policy, which permits investment in a marketplace model, where the platform provides a technology facility for independent sellers, but not in an inventory-led model where the platform owns the goods it sells (Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade, n.d.). The practical consequence is significant for the work described in this report: because the platform does not own the stock, growth in selection can only come from recruiting sellers, which is precisely why supply-side business development matters as much as it does.

A second layer governs conduct towards customers. The consumer protection rules for electronic commerce place duties on marketplace entities covering the disclosure of seller particulars, accuracy of product information, grievance handling and the prohibition of unfair trade practices (Ministry of Consumer Affairs, n.d.). Read alongside the packaged commodities rules, which require declarations of quantity, manufacturer and retail price on pre-packaged goods (Legal Metrology Division, n.d.), this explains why catalogue accuracy is a compliance matter and not merely a matter of presentation. A listing that misstates a pack size or omits a mandatory declaration is a regulatory exposure as well as a customer disappointment.

Category-specific licensing adds a further requirement. Food businesses selling online must hold the appropriate licence or registration, and platforms are expected to verify that their food sellers do (Food Safety and Standards Authority of India, n.d.), which is directly relevant to onboarding in the fast-moving consumer goods categories I worked on. Tax obligations also fall on the operator rather than only on the seller, since a platform is required to collect tax at source on the consideration it handles (Goods and Services Tax Network, n.d.).

Finally, the policy environment is actively encouraging open digital commerce, through a network intended to let buyers and sellers transact across platforms rather than within a single one (Open Network for Digital Commerce, n.d.). For a smaller platform this cuts both ways: it lowers the cost of reaching buyers, and it weakens the advantage of having recruited a seller exclusively. Either way it reinforces the lesson of the internship, that a marketplace competes on the quality of its supply relationships rather than on the existence of its website.

### 2.8  KEY OBSERVATIONS FROM THE JOB

The clearest observation from the internship is that a marketplace grows one seller at a time. No amount of research produces supply by itself; a person has to visit the distributor, explain the proposition, answer the objections and then follow up until the listing is live. The effort per seller is high, which is why category selection matters so much: it determines whether that effort is spent where demand exists.

A second observation is that data accuracy is a commercial matter rather than an administrative one. A wrong price, a missing pack size or an incomplete description is not an internal error; it is visible to every customer who opens the listing, and it costs the platform credibility with both sides of the market.

A third observation concerns price transparency in fast-moving consumer goods. Customers usually know what a familiar product costs, so a platform has very little room to be casual about pricing. This is why benchmarking recurred through the internship and why it was treated as an input to listing decisions.

Finally, I observed how closely the functions of a young company depend on one another. A defect noticed during field research became a report to the development team; a listing standard set by the platform determined what data I had to collect from a seller; a marketing poster created visibility for listings that the business development team had spent weeks securing. In a small organisation these connections are visible in a way they are not in a large one.


### 2.9  CHALLENGES ENCOUNTERED DURING THE INTERNSHIP

The internship also presented practical difficulties, each of which contributed to the learning.


#### 2.9.1  Seller Hesitation and Non-Response

Many sellers and distributors approached in the field were unfamiliar with selling through a platform and were cautious about committing, while others did not respond to follow-up at all. I learned to treat this as normal rather than as failure, to keep a record of every approach and its status, and to continue adding prospects while pursuing the existing ones.


#### 2.9.2  Incomplete Product Information

Product data obtained from sellers was frequently incomplete, with missing pack sizes, descriptions or images, which is precisely why the validation stage existed. I learned to identify what was missing before it reached a listing and to go back to the source rather than fill a gap by assumption.


#### 2.9.3  Price Volatility

Prices in fast-moving consumer goods change with promotions and stock positions, so a benchmark collected one week could be out of date the next. This is what taught me to date every observation and to repeat the comparison rather than rely on a single exercise.


#### 2.9.4  Continuity Across the Examination Break

The internship included a planned break at the end of May for the college semester examinations, in the middle of an active data collection exercise. Completing the validation of the data collected up to that point, and leaving clear notes on what remained, was what allowed the work to resume without loss when I returned.


#### 2.9.5  Coordinating Platform Defects with the Development Team

Reporting a website defect usefully proved harder than finding one. A description such as a page not working is of little use to a developer, and I learned to record what I had done, what I expected and what actually happened, and then to follow the report through until it was resolved.


### 2.10  OVERALL JOB EXPERIENCE

Taken as a whole, the internship gave me exposure across the full supply side of an online selling platform rather than depth in a single task. Category research, price benchmarking, listing preparation, data validation, field outreach, vendor meetings, proposal writing, marketing creatives and defect reporting are usually handled by different people in a larger company; working on all of them showed me how they fit together.

### 2.12  RECORD-KEEPING AND DATA DISCIPLINE

A theme that ran through every assignment, and one I had not expected to matter as much as it did, was the standard of record-keeping behind each output. A price is not information until its source, date and pack size are recorded with it, and a seller conversation is not progress until its status and next action are written down. That principle governed how I was asked to work, and I learned it mostly by getting it wrong first.

For price benchmarking it meant recording, against every observation, where the price had been seen, on what date and for what pack size. In a category where rates move weekly, an unsourced price is worthless a fortnight later, and I discovered this by having to repeat work I had already done. For product data it meant validating a specification against the seller’s own material rather than inferring it, and correcting at source rather than filling a gap by assumption.

For seller outreach it meant keeping a record of what each party had actually undertaken and when the next contact was due. This mattered most across the examination break, when work I had left had to be resumed weeks later, and it was the clearest demonstration I received that documentation is a courtesy to colleagues rather than an administrative imposition.


### 2.13  CONDUCT IN THE FIELD

Representing a platform in person to sellers and distributors brought obligations that desk work does not. In a field conversation an intern is, for practical purposes, the company: what I said about commission, onboarding timelines or catalogue requirements would be remembered as the platform’s position, and an overstatement made to secure interest would become a problem for somebody else later.

The rule I was given, and came to appreciate, was to promise nothing I could not confirm. Where a seller asked about terms I was not authorised to settle, the correct answer was that I would have it confirmed and come back, which is less impressive in the moment and considerably better afterwards. I also learned to be accurate about what the platform could not yet do, since a seller who discovers a limitation after onboarding is harder to retain than one who was told about it in advance.

Conduct also meant respecting the seller’s time and circumstances. A distributor running a counter has customers ahead of a visitor with a proposition, and learning to read when to wait, when to leave material and when to return was a practical skill rather than a courtesy. Commercial information shared in confidence, such as a seller’s margins or his terms with another platform, stayed within the team.


### 2.14  COORDINATION ACROSS TEAMS

A single piece of supply-side work frequently involved three teams, and learning to move between them was part of the job. A listing problem could require the business development team to obtain corrected data from the seller, the marketing team to revise a creative, and the developers to fix a defect that prevented a field from saving correctly.

The practical lesson was about translation. A seller describes a problem in commercial terms, a developer needs it in reproducible terms, and a marketing colleague needs to know only what changes on the customer-facing side. Writing a defect report that stated what I did, what happened and what should have happened saved a round of questions, and I learned to do that after several reports that did not.

The second lesson was about sequence. In a small organisation people are shared rather than dedicated, so a request that arrives without context waits. Explaining why a correction mattered, and what it unblocked, consistently produced faster help than a bare request did. That is an unglamorous observation, and it was one of the more useful ones I took from the twelve weeks.


### 2.15  CONCLUSION OF JOB / TASK DESCRIPTION

The work described in this chapter covered the complete sequence by which an online selling platform builds supply: choosing a category, establishing competitive prices, finding and persuading sellers, collecting and validating product data, publishing listings, promoting them and improving the platform on which they sit.

It also allowed me to apply concepts from marketing management, market research, sales and distribution, pricing and business communication to live commercial work, and to learn the systems and formats on which a small e-commerce team depends. The tasks were varied in form but consistent in their demand, since each of them ended either in a listing that a customer would see or in a conversation with a seller who had to be convinced.

Most importantly, the chapter reflects a progression. The work I was given in the first week was research under close supervision; by the closing weeks I was drafting proposals, managing partner correspondence and following onboarding conversations through to closure. That progression is the clearest measure of what the internship achieved.

One further observation belongs here, because it shaped how I understood the whole assignment. Every task I was given served one of three ends: deciding where supply effort should be spent, making the platform’s offer credible to a seller, or making the catalogue trustworthy to a customer. Category research and benchmarking served the first, outreach and proposals the second, and data validation, listing discipline and defect reporting the third. Recognising that structure was what turned eight apparently separate work areas into a single understanding of the business, and it is the reason I could see, by the closing weeks, why a correction to a pack size mattered as much as a meeting with a distributor.

Taken together, the twelve weeks moved from researching a category on a spreadsheet to persuading a distributor in his own shop and then listing his products accurately enough for a stranger to buy them with confidence. That sequence, from analysis through persuasion to execution, is the supply side of a marketplace in miniature, and performing all three parts of it is what the internship taught me that no case study had.

What I carry forward from the chapter is less the list of tasks than the judgement that sat behind them: which category is worth the effort, which seller is worth a third visit, and which detail in a listing a customer will actually notice. Those are decisions rather than procedures, and learning to make them is what separated the last fortnight of the internship from the first.

| Business Communication | Business proposals by email, follow-up correspondence and internal project reports |
| E-Commerce and Operations | Catalogue management, listing standards, data validation and platform defect reporting |
| Organisational Behaviour | Working across business development, marketing and technology teams in a small organisation |

The internship also exposed the limits of purely academic preparation. Coursework describes a distribution channel as a diagram; practice required me to find the distributor, travel to the shop, explain the platform and return three times before anything happened. That gap between a described channel and a built one is, in my view, the real content of an internship.


### 4.6  OVERALL REALISATIONS

A marketplace is built one seller at a time, and the effort per seller is high, which makes category selection the most consequential decision. Data without a source and a date is not evidence, and in a price-sensitive category it is actively dangerous. Accuracy in a listing is a commercial act, because it is the only thing a customer sees of all the work behind it. Non-response is the normal condition of outreach, so volume, records and patience are part of the method. A small organisation makes the connections between functions visible, which is the fastest way to understand how a business actually works. Platform quality and business development are the same project: sellers will not stay on a platform that customers do not trust. Being able to do both desk analysis and field work is more valuable than being excellent at only one of them.


### 4.7  PROFESSIONAL INSIGHTS AND LEARNINGS

Three insights from the internship will stay with me beyond the technical content.

The first concerns the nature of early-stage business. A young platform does not win by having a better idea; it wins by doing the unglamorous supply work that larger competitors do not bother to do in a particular category or locality. Almost everything I was asked to do was an instance of that, and seeing it up close has changed how I read company stories.

The second concerns learning inside an organisation. I learned most of what I know now by being given real work early, attempting it and having it corrected in team reviews. Feedback on my listings, reports and creatives told me precisely where my judgement was weak, and being willing to be corrected turned out to be the most efficient learning strategy available.

The third concerns my own career direction. Before the internship my interest in management was general. Having worked through research, benchmarking, field outreach, proposals and onboarding, I now know that I am drawn to business development and e-commerce operations, where the work combines analysis with direct commercial contact. I also know the gaps I need to close, namely negotiation, analytical depth and prospect qualification, and I have a clear idea of how to work on them.


### 4.8  RELEVANCE TO FUTURE CAREER AND FURTHER DEVELOPMENT

The internship has a direct bearing on the path I intend to follow. The work I performed is the work of the early years of a career in business development and e-commerce operations, and having done it in the field rather than in a case study I now know both that I can do it and that I enjoy it. That is a more useful conclusion than the general interest in marketing I had before.

The transferable part is not the FMCG categories but the method: researching a market before approaching it, benchmarking with the source and date recorded, preparing data that somebody else will rely on, making a proposition in person, and following a conversation through to a decision. Those apply in any supply-side or sales role, and they are what a first professional position would expect from the outset.

What I still need is equally clear. Negotiating commercial terms, reading platform analytics, understanding the unit economics of an order, and the warehousing and logistics side of fulfilment are all areas where I have observation without practice. Those gaps are why I intend to seek a first role that exposes me to the commercial and analytical side of a marketplace rather than to supply alone.


### 4.9  UNDERSTANDING OF THE MARKETPLACE BUSINESS

A less obvious outcome was a working understanding of how a marketplace actually works, which I did not have before. A platform does not own most of what it sells; it earns by assembling supply, presenting it accurately and pricing it competitively, and each of those three is a distinct operational problem with its own failure modes.

That structure explains why the work I was given took the form it did. Category research decides where supply effort is spent, because a platform cannot recruit sellers in every category at once. Price benchmarking exists because a customer can compare in seconds, so a price that is out of line is not a lost margin but a lost visit. Catalogue accuracy matters because a listing is the only thing a customer sees, and an error in it is visible to everyone who searches. Seller outreach exists because none of the preceding work has any value until somebody agrees to list.

Seeing the business in those terms changed how I read my own tasks. Correcting a pack size stopped being data entry and became the removal of a reason for a customer to distrust the platform, which is a considerably more useful way to think about the work.

The broader realisation is that an e-commerce platform is a business development operation with a technology interface attached, rather than a technology business that happens to sell things. Everything in the catalogue is there because somebody found a seller, earned a meeting, corrected the data and followed the conversation through to a listing. Having done each of those things myself, I now read a product page as the end of a process rather than as a page.

That change of perspective is, I think, the most durable thing the internship gave me.

# CHAPTER 5 — SUMMARY AND CONCLUSION


## 5. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSION


### 5.1  SUMMARY OF INTERNSHIP EXPERIENCE

The Summer Internship Programme was carried out at Q-Up Private Limited, an online selling platform, on-site, from 4 May 2026 to 31 July 2026, as a Business Development and Operations Intern under the guidance of **Ms. Saranya Selvapandian**. The twelve-week programme covered the entire supply side of a marketplace, from category research to seller onboarding.

The internship began with product category research, in which I identified and finalised the fast-moving consumer goods categories to be taken up for onboarding and collected preliminary market data for them. It moved into price comparison across retail outlets and e-commerce platforms, and the preparation of product listings for the platform, followed by detailed product and market research documented in project reports and participation in a promotional video shoot.

The middle weeks concentrated on supply. After a planned break at the end of May for the college semester examinations, I resumed product data collection and took part in internal reviews of project progress. I then researched potential FMCG sellers and distributors, conducted field visits to approach them, attended vendor meetings and partnership discussions, and drafted professional emails for business proposals while following up with prospective clients.

The later weeks combined marketing with platform work. I carried out additional price benchmarking and market analysis, designed promotional posters and marketing creatives, identified and reported website defects to the development team, conducted field research on customer and seller needs, updated product listings, and in the closing weeks followed up with clients through to seller onboarding while managing the correspondence with prospective partners.

The work was carried out on the Q-Up platform itself, with spreadsheets for category research, product data and price comparison, design software for promotional creatives, and email for business correspondence, supported by the organisation’s project reporting formats and internal team reviews.


### 5.2  KEY TAKEAWAYS


#### 5.2.1  Understanding of the Marketplace Model

Learned that a platform’s growth depends on supply, and that supply is built seller by seller through research, outreach and follow-up. Understood how category selection determines where the whole supply-side effort is spent. Saw how catalogue depth and price credibility together decide whether a customer returns.


#### 5.2.2  Research and Benchmarking

Gained the ability to evaluate a category on demand and onboarding potential rather than on size alone. Learned to benchmark prices across retail and e-commerce sources and to record source, date and pack size against every observation. Learned to convert raw research into a structured project report that others can act on.


#### 5.2.3  Field Business Development

Gained real experience of approaching FMCG sellers and distributors in person and explaining a commercial proposition. Participated in vendor meetings and partnership discussions and observed how terms are negotiated. Learned that onboarding is the close of a sales process and usually requires several contacts.


#### 5.2.4  Data Accuracy and Catalogue Discipline

Learned to validate product data before it is used for onboarding or listing. Understood that a listing error is visible to every customer and costs the platform credibility with both sides of the market. Developed the habit of correcting at source rather than filling gaps by assumption.


#### 5.2.5  Communication and Follow-Up

Improved professional written communication through business proposals and partner correspondence. Learned to keep a request alive courteously and to record what each party had undertaken. Learned to describe a platform defect in terms a development team can act upon.


#### 5.2.6  Cross-Team Working and Self-Management

Saw how business development, marketing and technology depend on one another in a small organisation. Learned to move between analysis, field work and creative work within the same week. Learned to leave work in a state that allows it to be resumed, which the examination break tested directly.


#### 5.2.7  Handling Refusal and Non-Response

Learned that in field business development most approaches do not succeed, and that treating a non-committal answer as a refusal wastes the research that earned the meeting. Understood that persistence has to be courteous and spaced, because the difference between diligent follow-up and nuisance is judged entirely by the person receiving it. Developed the habit of recording what each party had actually undertaken, so that a later conversation could resume from fact rather than from memory.


#### 5.2.8  Continuity and Handover

Understood, through an interruption I had not planned for, how much of what I knew about a set of prospects existed only in my own head. Learned to leave work in a state that allows somebody else to continue it, which means recording status, next action and the reason for both. Came to see documentation as a courtesy to colleagues rather than as an administrative requirement imposed on the work.


#### 5.2.9  Representing the Organisation

Learned that in a field conversation an intern is, for practical purposes, the company, and that anything said about commission or timelines will be remembered as the platform’s position. Understood that promising nothing one cannot confirm is less impressive in the moment and far better afterwards. Learned to be candid about what the platform could not yet do, since a seller who discovers a limitation after onboarding is harder to keep.


### 5.3  LIMITATIONS OF THE INTERNSHIP EXPERIENCE

An honest summary has to record what the internship did not reach. Twelve weeks on the supply side of one platform gives a detailed view of seller acquisition and catalogue work rather than a complete picture of an e-commerce business, and much of the platform lay outside my work.

My assignments were concentrated on supply: category research, price benchmarking, listings, seller outreach and onboarding. I did not work on the demand side at all. Customer acquisition, retention, the economics of a delivery, returns handling and customer service were visible to me only as topics other people discussed. Nor did I see warehousing or logistics operations, which is a substantial gap in my understanding of how an order is actually fulfilled after a listing converts.

I also saw onboarding conversations rather than their conclusions. Commercial terms, margin structures and contracts were settled above my level, and while I prepared proposals and followed prospects up, I was not present when terms were negotiated. My exposure to the platform’s own numbers was similarly limited: I reported defects and prepared listings without visibility of traffic, conversion or revenue.

Two practical limits are worth recording as well. My field visits covered sellers and distributors within reach of Chennai, so the report describes one urban market and not the national picture. And the planned break at the end of May for my semester examinations interrupted the work, which taught me about handover but did mean I saw two shorter stretches rather than one continuous engagement.


### 5.4  RECOMMENDATIONS


#### 5.4.1  Suggestions to the Organisation

The following are offered respectfully and from a trainee’s vantage point. They are not claims that the organisation lacks these practices, and each would need to fit its existing systems and priorities.

- A single template for the seller pitch, covering commission, onboarding steps, catalogue requirements and settlement timelines, would let a new team member explain the proposition consistently instead of assembling the answer from several conversations.
- Recording the source and date against every benchmarked price would make a comparison sheet reusable, since an unsourced price is of little value a fortnight later in a category where rates move.
- A short catalogue standard, setting out the mandatory fields, image requirements and pack-size conventions for FMCG listings, would reduce the correction work that incomplete seller data currently generates.
- A simple shared tracker of seller conversations, with status and next action, would prevent a prospect from going quiet unnoticed when the person following them up is away.


#### 5.4.2  Suggestions for Future Interns

For a student about to undertake similar work, four things would have helped me had I known them at the start.

- Learn the category before approaching the seller. A distributor can tell within a minute whether you understand his margins, and the conversation changes completely once he decides that you do.
- Record every price with its source, date and pack size at the moment you capture it. Reconstructing that context afterwards is slow and usually impossible.
- Expect to return. An onboarding is rarely settled at a first visit, and treating a non-committal answer as a refusal wastes the research that earned the meeting.
- Write the defect report the way a developer needs it: what you did, what happened and what should have happened. A vague report costs a round trip that a precise one avoids.


### 5.5  PERSONAL REFLECTION

On a personal level the internship was the first work I have done where the outcome depended on persuading a stranger. Walking into a distributor’s shop with a proposition, being told to come back another day, and returning a third time is a particular kind of education, and it is not one a classroom can simulate. I was uncomfortable with it in the first fortnight and had stopped minding by the sixth week.

What changed most was my attitude to detail. I began by treating a listing as a form to be completed and came to see it as a public statement the platform makes about a product, visible to every customer who searches for it. Once that reframing happened, checking a pack size or a price stopped feeling like an imposition and started feeling like the substance of the job.

The examination break taught me something unplanned. Handing work over and picking it up again weeks later showed me exactly how much of what I knew was in my head rather than in my records, and it was more than I had assumed. I returned with the habit of writing things down so that somebody else could continue them, which is probably the most transferable thing I took from the whole internship.


### 5.6  CONCLUSION

The internship at Q-Up Private Limited was the point at which my study of management became practical. Over twelve weeks I moved from researching product categories to benchmarking prices, preparing listings, visiting sellers and distributors, taking part in partnership discussions, producing promotional material, reporting platform defects and following onboarding conversations through to closure. In doing so I acquired skills that are directly employable: category and market research, price benchmarking, catalogue preparation, field business development, proposal writing and client follow-up.

Beyond the technical content, three things changed. First, my understanding of e-commerce: a marketplace is a business development operation with a technology interface, and its catalogue exists because somebody did the supply work. Second, my understanding of data: a number is only useful when its source, date and definition are recorded, and in a price-transparent category that discipline is commercial rather than academic. Third, my understanding of myself. I learned that I am comfortable approaching businesses I do not know, that I can sustain follow-up through non-response, and that I enjoy work which combines analysis with direct commercial contact.

I am also clear about what remains to be developed: negotiation of commercial terms, analytical depth in pricing and category profitability, stronger spreadsheet and design technique, and better qualification of prospects so that effort is concentrated where it is most likely to convert. Knowing these gaps precisely, rather than in general terms, is itself an outcome of the internship.

In conclusion, the internship achieved what a Summer Internship Programme is intended to achieve. It connected the MBA curriculum to live commercial work, it gave me practical experience of the supply side of an online selling platform, and it settled my career direction towards business development and e-commerce operations. I am grateful to the management of Q-Up Private Limited for the opportunity, to **Ms. Saranya Selvapandian** for her guidance and review, and to the business development, operations, marketing and technology teams for their support, and I leave the organisation with both the competence and the confidence to contribute to a professional business development team.

I leave the organisation with an accurate rather than an inflated sense of what I can do, a specific list of what I still need to learn, and the settled intention to build my career in business development and e-commerce operations.

I am grateful to the organisation for sending a trainee into the field rather than keeping him at a desk, because the field is where the lessons were.
